Studio 7 playbook

Build it. Hand it over. They run it.

You build websites for local businesses that don't have one. The goal is to finish each job cleanly so the owner can manage the site without you. Three parts, in order.

Part 1Before you talk to anyoneDefine it, price it, get paid, set up email, write your process. Part 2Set up your shopBuild your site and three demos, pick your URL, launch. Part 3Run the businessFind customers, pitch, build, hand over.
Don't contact a customer until the ready check at the end of Part 2 is fully ticked.

Part 1

Before you talk to anyone

Settle all of this first. Plan on about a week.

Six sections Your rules Done check
  1. 1.1Define Studio 7One page. Say it in 20 seconds.
    • Who: local small businesses with no website. Start with restaurants and food trucks.
    • Problem: customers can't find hours, a menu or a location online, so they go somewhere else.
    • Promise: a real website, built fast, handed over completely, that you can update yourself.
    • You don't do: ongoing maintenance, logos, photography, online stores, payment systems.

    Write it in your own words and make it the first page of your Studio 7 folder. Restaurants and food trucks fit well because a menu in a Google Sheet and a schedule in Google Calendar are what the Premium package is made for.

    Saying no is easier when it's written down. Owners will ask for logos, photos and "one quick tweak next month."

  3. 1.3How you get paidYour dad signs. The money goes to his account.

    Why a parent signs

    In most places a contract with someone under 18 can be cancelled by the minor. A customer could walk away and you'd have little recourse. Payment services also tend to need an adult owner. Stripe, for example, says that under 18 a legal guardian must own the account before it can accept charges (Stripe's age rule).

    Studio 7 is the name you work under. Your dad is the contracting party.

    Set it up

    1. Your dad opens an invoicing account in his name. Square Invoices has a free plan in the US with unlimited invoices and deposits (Square's pricing). Check the current terms when you sign up.
    2. Send yourself or your dad a test invoice so you've seen what a customer sees. Don't charge it.
    3. Decide how fees are handled. On the free plan, a card payment on an invoice costs 3.3% plus 30 cents. That's about $16.80 on a $500 invoice and $33.30 on $1,000. The simplest choice is to treat it as a cost of doing business and leave your prices as they are.

    Payment rules

    • 50% deposit before work starts. 50% before you hand over.
    • Every payment goes through an invoice, so there's a record.
    • A receipt for every payment. No cash without one.
    • Pick your refund rule before the first sale. A common one: the deposit is non-refundable once work starts.
    • One folder per customer: signed agreement, intake form, approval email, invoices, handoff checklist, owner's guide.
    This isn't legal or tax advice. Your dad should check whether a Washington business license or local registration is needed, and how the income is reported for taxes.
  4. 1.4Set up your business emailA separate address used only for Studio 7.
    1. Create a new free email account just for the business. Keep your personal one for personal things.
    2. Pick a plain name. Something like studio7web or finn.studio7. If it's taken, add a word, not a string of numbers.
    3. Turn on two-step verification and set your dad's phone as the recovery option. Never share the password with anyone else.
    4. Add a signature: your name, Studio 7, and your website link once it's live.
    5. Make two labels: Prospects and Customers.
    6. Use this address for everything: pitches, customers, your GitHub account, the invoicing account's customer contact.

    Later, once you have your own domain, you can add an address on it, such as hello@yourdomain. That's optional and it can wait until after your first sale.

  5. 1.5Your process, pitch to deliveryEight steps. Know them before you start.

    2. Make a concept preview (you)
    3. Send the pitch (you)
    4. Sign and deposit (your dad signs, the owner pays 50%)
    5. Build and check (you)

    Part 3 has the details for each step. Write the list on one page, put it in your folder, and be able to say it from memory.

    A rule that makes this work: you never skip a step because a customer is in a hurry. The order is what protects you.

Set up your shop

Build your own site and three demo sites, then put them online. This is your practice run and your proof.

Four sections Ready check
  1. 2.1Build Studio 7's own siteOne page, built the way you'll build for customers.

    Owners will check whether you have a website. Build it as a Starter-level page and run your build check on it before it goes live.

    What goes on it

    • One sentence at the top about what Studio 7 does and for whom
    • A main button: "Get a free concept preview"
    • The three packages with prices
    • Links to your demo sites, each labeled as a sample
    • A short FAQ: what's editable, who owns the site, what the 30-day window covers
    • Your business email as a link that opens a new message

    Use an email link instead of a contact form. A form on a static site needs an outside service to deliver the messages, and you don't need that yet.

    Build a single-page website for Studio 7 as one self-contained HTML file. Studio 7 is a one-person business that builds websites for local small businesses that don't have one.

Facts: [my business email, the area I work in, the three packages and prices]

Rules:
- Use only the facts above. Don't invent clients, testimonials, awards, or results.
- Top of the page: one sentence saying what Studio 7 does and for whom.
- Main button: "Get a free concept preview". It opens an email to [my business email].
- Show the three packages with prices and what's included.
- Add a short FAQ: what the owner can edit, who owns the site, what the 30-day fix window covers.
- Link to the three demo sites and label each "Sample, fictional business".
- Design for phones first. Keep it simple.
- At the end, list every assumption you made.
    No fake testimonials or made-up client logos. When a real customer is happy, ask for a quote in writing and add it then.
  2. 2.2Build three demo sitesFictional businesses, one for each package.

    Demos show owners what they'd get, and building them is how you learn the process before it costs you anything. Make one at each level so your site shows the whole ladder.

    Premium demoA taco truck

    Menu in a Google Sheet, daily location in a Google Calendar. Edit both and watch the site change.

    Pro demoA barbershop

    Home, services and prices, about, contact. Four pages.

    Starter demoA neighborhood bakery

    One page: hours, map, a few placeholder photos.

    Rules for demos

    • Invent the business. Search the name first to make sure it isn't a real business near you.
    • Use made-up details: an address like 123 Sample Street and a phone number from 555-0100 to 555-0199, which the US sets aside for fiction.
    • Put a visible banner on every page: "Sample site for a fictional business."
    • Use placeholder images, or images you made. No photos of real places or people.
    • For the taco truck, create the Sheet and Calendar yourself and test an edit. This is how you learn Premium.
    • Run your build check on each demo.
    Build a single-page sample website as one self-contained HTML file for a fictional business.

Business: [made-up name]
Type: [food truck / barbershop / bakery]
Made-up facts: [address like 123 Sample Street, phone 555-0123, hours, menu or services]

Rules:
- Use only the facts above. Don't use real business names, real addresses, or real people.
- Put a visible banner at the top: "Sample site for a fictional business".
- Use placeholder images, not photos of real places or people.
- Design for phones first, then scale up.
- Keep the look simple and warm, matching the business type.
- At the end, list every assumption you made.
  3. 2.3Pick your URLShort, easy to say, bought by your dad.
    1. Write a short list of names you like. The name Studio 7 may be taken, so have versions that add a word, such as studio7web or studio7sites. Check availability before you get attached.
    2. Prefer a .com. Skip hyphens and numbers beyond the 7.
    3. The phone test: if you say it out loud once, could someone type it correctly?
    4. Search the name online to make sure another business isn't already using it.
    5. Your dad buys it at a domain registrar, in his name. Look at the renewal price and not just the first-year price. A .com is typically about $10 to $20 a year (approximate).
    6. Turn on auto-renew. Your dad keeps the registrar login.

    If you don't have the domain yet, launch anyway on the free GitHub address and add the domain later.

  4. 2.4Launch on GitHub PagesFree hosting. About an hour, plus waiting for DNS.
    1. Create a GitHub account with your business email. GitHub requires users to be at least 13 (GitHub's terms).
    2. Create a new public repository. Add your site as index.html in the main folder, and each demo in its own folder, such as demos/taco-truck/index.html. Commit to main.
    3. Open the repository's Settings, then Pages. Set the source to "Deploy from a branch", pick main and the root folder, and save. In a few minutes the site is live at a github.io address.
    4. To use your own domain, add it in Settings, then Pages, first. Do this before you change any DNS records, or someone else could claim it.
    5. At your registrar's DNS settings, remove the default records, then add four A records for the main domain: 185.199.108.153, 185.199.109.153, 185.199.110.153 and 185.199.111.153. Add a CNAME record for www that points to your username.github.io address.
    6. Wait. DNS can take up to 24 hours. When the option appears, tick "Enforce HTTPS".
    7. Don't use wildcard DNS records. GitHub warns they create a takeover risk.
    8. Test on your phone and your laptop: every link, every demo, the email button.
    GitHub changes its screens now and then. The official custom domain guide is the source if something looks different. GitHub Pages sites are public, so don't put anything private in the repository.

The pitch

Send this from your business email with the concept preview attached as a PDF.

Hi, I'm Finn. I build websites for local businesses. I noticed [Business] doesn't have one yet, so I made a quick concept of what yours could look like. It's attached as a PDF.

If you like the direction, I can build the real thing, set it up in your name, and show you how to update it yourself. No pressure either way.
"I already have Instagram."
That's great for followers. A website is where people check your hours and menu before they visit.
"That's too much money."
The Starter is $500 for one clean page. Want me to quote that instead?
"I'll think about it."
Of course. I'll keep the concept for two weeks. Want me to check in then?
"My cousin could do it."
Maybe so. I'd want to be sure it's finished and handed over with instructions. That's what you get from me.
"How would I update it?"
Be exact. Premium: through a Google Sheet or Calendar. Starter and Pro: you order a change from me, quoted up front.

The owner's guide

This is what lets you walk away.

Why it matters

Without a guide, every question is a text to you. With one, the owner has the answer at 9 PM on a Sunday when you're not around.

It's the difference between selling a website and selling a website you have to keep supporting.

What goes in it

  • Where to edit: the exact link and tab
  • The common changes, one per page, with screenshots
  • How long a change takes to show (test it first)
  • What never to touch: column headers, tab names, column order
  • What to do if something looks wrong, and who to call
  • Who owns what
  • How to ask you for a change, and when the fix window ends
Test it first. Hand the guide to someone who's never seen the site and ask them to change a price using only the guide. Fix every spot where they get stuck. Save it as a PDF in the customer's folder.
